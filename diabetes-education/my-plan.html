    About 1 in 10 people in the United States has diabetes. Most of them have type 2. Many live long, full, active lives. With the right tools and support, you can too.

    Part 2 · About 7 minutes

    What is type 2 diabetes?

    Goals for this part
    • Explain how insulin and blood sugar work
    • Know what the A1C test tells you

    Food becomes fuel

    When you eat, your body breaks food down into a sugar called glucose. Glucose travels in your blood. It is the main fuel for your body.

    An organ called the pancreas makes insulin. Insulin works like a key. It opens the "doors" of your cells so glucose can get in and give you energy.

    What goes wrong in type 2

    In type 2 diabetes, two things happen:

    1. The locks get "sticky." Cells do not respond well to insulin. This is called insulin resistance.
    2. The pancreas gets tired. Over time it cannot make enough insulin to keep up.

    So glucose stays in the blood. Too much glucose for too long can harm blood vessels and nerves.

    The A1C test

    The A1C is a blood test that shows your average blood sugar over about 3 months. For many adults, the goal is below 7%. Your care team will help set the right goal for you.

    What A1C means as an average blood sugar
    A1CAbout the same as an average blood sugar of
    6%126 mg/dL
    7%154 mg/dL (a common goal)
    8%183 mg/dL
    9%212 mg/dL
    10%240 mg/dL
    Good news Every step toward your goal counts. Lowering your A1C even a little lowers your risk of eye, kidney, and nerve problems.

    Part 3 · About 8 minutes

    Knowing your numbers

    Goals for this part
    • Know common blood sugar targets
    • Know when to check and what your number means

    Checking your blood sugar is like looking at a map. It shows where you are, so you can choose where to go next. A number is information, not a grade.

    Two ways to check

    Blood sugar meter

    A tiny drop of blood from the side of your fingertip. Shows your number right now.

    Continuous glucose monitor (CGM)

    A small sensor worn on the arm or belly. Checks every few minutes and sends your numbers to a phone or reader. Shows trends with arrows.

    Common targets for adults

    CheckCommon goal
    Before meals80–130 mg/dL
    1–2 hours after you start eatingLess than 180 mg/dL
    A1CLess than 7%
    CGM "time in range" (70–180 mg/dL)More than 70% of the day (about 17 hours)
    CGM time below 70 mg/dLLess than 4% of the day (about 1 hour)
    Your goals may be different Older adults and people with other health problems often have safer, higher goals. Write your personal goals here and ask your care team.

    When to check

    Your care team will tell you how often. Common times are:

    • When you wake up, before eating
    • Before meals, or 2 hours after a meal
    • Any time you feel shaky, sweaty, or "not right"
    • When you are sick
    • Before driving, if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea pill

    Fingerstick tips

    1. Wash hands with soap and warm water. Dry them well. (Sugar on your fingers can give a false high.)
    2. Use the side of your fingertip. It hurts less.
    3. Write down your number, or let your meter or app save it.
    4. Never share your lancet or meter.

    Part 4 · About 11 minutes

    Healthy, plant-forward eating

    Goals for this part
    • Use the plate method
    • Know which foods have carbs
    • Read a food label

    There is no single "diabetes diet." Many eating styles can work. What matters is a pattern you enjoy and can keep.

    Eating patterns built on plants — vegetables, beans, lentils, whole grains, fruit, nuts, and seeds — can help lower blood sugar, support a healthy weight, and protect your heart. You do not have to give up all meat or dairy. Just let plants fill more of your plate.

    The plate method

    Use a 9-inch plate (about the length of a dollar bill across).

    • Half the plate: non-starchy vegetables (greens, broccoli, peppers, carrots, tomatoes, green beans)
    • One quarter: protein (beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, fish, chicken)
    • One quarter: carbohydrate foods (brown rice, whole grain bread, sweet potato, corn, fruit)
    • Drink: water or another zero-calorie drink
    Try it

    Build your plate

    Know your carbs

    Carbohydrates ("carbs") raise blood sugar the most. You still need them for energy. The key is the type and the amount.

    Foods with carbs

    Bread, rice, pasta, tortillas, cereal, potatoes, corn, beans and lentils, fruit and juice, milk and yogurt, sweets, and sugary drinks.

    Choose more often

    High-fiber carbs: beans, lentils, oats, brown rice, whole grain bread, whole fruit, and vegetables. Fiber slows the rise in blood sugar.

    About 15 grams of carbs is in: 1 slice of bread · ⅓ cup cooked rice or pasta · 1 small piece of fruit · ½ cup of beans (which also give you protein and fiber).

    Drinks

    • Best: water, sparkling water, unsweetened tea, or coffee with little or no sugar.
    • Save for now and then: regular soda, sweet tea, energy drinks, and juice. One 20-ounce soda has about 16 teaspoons of sugar, so swapping even one a day makes a real difference.
    • Alcohol: if you drink, talk with your care team first. Alcohol can cause low blood sugar, especially with insulin or sulfonylurea pills.

    Small changes add up For people with extra weight, losing just 5–7% of body weight can lower blood sugar. For someone who weighs 200 pounds, that is 10 to 14 pounds.

    Part 5 · About 6 minutes

    Moving more

    Goals for this part
    • Know the activity goal
    • Stay safe while active

    Moving your body helps insulin work better. It can lower blood sugar for hours. It also helps your heart, mood, sleep, and weight.

    Goals for most adults

    • 150 minutes a week of activity that makes you breathe a little harder — like a brisk walk. That is 30 minutes, 5 days a week.
    • Try not to go more than 2 days in a row without activity.
    • Strength activities 2–3 times a week — like resistance bands, light weights, or standing up from a chair 10 times.
    • Break up sitting every 30 minutes. Stand, stretch, or walk for a few minutes.
    A simple tip A 10–15 minute walk after meals can help lower the rise in blood sugar after eating.

    Start where you are. If 5 minutes is what you can do today, start with 5. Add a little each week.

    Stay safe

    • Talk with your care team before starting a hard exercise program, especially if you have heart, eye, or foot problems.
    • If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea pill, check your blood sugar before activity and carry fast sugar (like glucose tablets).
    • Wear good shoes and socks. Check your feet after activity.
    • Drink water.
    • Stop and get help if you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or feel faint.
    Try it

    Plan your week

    How family can help Be an activity buddy. Walk together after dinner. Make it fun — put on music, explore a park, or play outside with the kids.
    Reflection moment · optional Rest is part of health, too Bodies need both movement and rest. Many people find that a weekly day of rest — such as a Sabbath — and time in nature lower stress and renew their spirit. Aim for 7–9 hours of sleep each night.

    Part 6 · About 8 minutes

    Taking your medicines

    Goals for this part
    • Know what your medicines do
    • Take them safely, every day

    Type 2 diabetes changes over time. Many people need medicine, and many need more over the years. That is not a failure. It is how the condition works. Medicine is a tool, just like food and activity.

    Common diabetes medicines

    TypeExamplesHow it helpsGood to know
    MetforminmetforminMakes the liver release less sugar. Helps insulin work better.Take with food. Upset stomach often gets better with time.
    GLP-1 medicinessemaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide, tirzepatide*Help the body release insulin after meals, slow digestion, and lower appetite.Most are weekly shots; one is a pill. May cause nausea at first. Some protect the heart and kidneys.
    SGLT2 inhibitorsempagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozinHelp the kidneys remove extra sugar in the urine.Protect the heart and kidneys. Drink water. Keep the genital area clean. May need to pause when sick.
    DPP-4 inhibitorssitagliptin, linagliptinHelp your body make more insulin after meals.Usually gentle, with few side effects.
    Sulfonylureasglipizide, glimepirideMake the pancreas release more insulin.Can cause low blood sugar. Regular meals help keep you steady.
    Insulinlong-acting and mealtime insulinsReplaces the insulin your body cannot make.Can cause low blood sugar. Very effective.

    *Tirzepatide works on both GLP-1 and GIP hormones. Names shown are generic names. Your brand name may be different — ask your pharmacist.

    Tips for taking medicines

    • Take them at the same time each day. Use a pill box or phone alarm.
    • Do not stop a medicine on your own. Talk with your care team first, even if you feel fine.
    • Tell your team about side effects. There are often other choices.
    • Keep an up-to-date list of all your medicines, vitamins, and herbs. Bring it to every visit.
    • Trouble paying? Tell your care team or pharmacist. There are often lower-cost options or help programs.

    If you use insulin or other shots

    • Keep unopened pens and vials in the refrigerator. Never freeze them or leave them in a hot car.
    • Once opened, most insulin can stay at room temperature for about 28 days. Check your label, because some are different.
    • Change where you inject each time (belly, thighs, back of arms) to keep the skin healthy.
    • Never share pens, even if you change the needle.
    • Put used needles in a hard "sharps" container.

    Match the medicine to what it does

    Part 7 · About 10 minutes · Most important for safety

    Lows, highs, and sick days

    Goals for this part
    • Spot and treat low blood sugar
    • Know what to do when blood sugar is high or you are sick
    • Know when to call your team and when to call 911

    Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia): below 70 mg/dL

    Lows happen most often with insulin or sulfonylurea pills. They can also happen after skipping a meal, more activity than usual, or drinking alcohol.

    Early signs

    Shaky · sweaty · fast heartbeat · hungry · dizzy · grumpy or anxious · headache

    Serious signs

    Confused · blurry vision · trouble talking or walking · very sleepy · seizure · passing out

    Treat a low with the Rule of 15

    1. Eat or drink 15 grams of fast sugar: 4 glucose tablets, OR ½ cup (4 oz) juice or regular soda, OR 1 tablespoon sugar or honey.
    2. Wait 15 minutes. Check again.
    3. Still below 70? Repeat steps 1 and 2.
    4. Back above 70? Eat a meal or snack if your next meal is more than an hour away.

    Choose fast sugar, like tablets or juice, to treat a low. Chocolate and other foods with fat work more slowly.

    Try it

    Walk through the Rule of 15

    Severe low — for family and friends If the person is passed out, having a seizure, or cannot swallow safely:
    • Do not put food or drink in their mouth.
    • Give glucagon if they have it (nose spray or ready-to-use shot). Turn them on their side.
    • Call 911.
    Ask the care team if a glucagon prescription is right for you, especially if you take insulin.
    Driving Do not drive with low blood sugar. If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, check before driving. If you are low, pull over, treat it, and wait until you feel normal and your number is back in range.

    High blood sugar (hyperglycemia)

    Signs: very thirsty · urinating a lot · tired · blurry vision · headache.

    Causes: more food or carbs than usual · missed medicine · less activity · stress · illness or infection · some medicines (like steroids).

    What to do: drink water · take your medicines as prescribed · take a walk if you feel well · check more often · look for a pattern. Call your team if it stays high.

    A dangerous high Very high blood sugar (often over 400–600), with extreme thirst, confusion, or very dry mouth can be a sign of a serious problem called HHS. Breathing fast, stomach pain, and vomiting can be signs of DKA. Both are emergencies. Get care right away.

    Sick-day rules

    Being sick (flu, infection, vomiting) often makes blood sugar go up, even if you are not eating.

    • Check blood sugar every 4 hours (or as your team says). Write it down.
    • Drink plenty of fluids — about 1 cup of water or sugar-free drink every hour you are awake.
    • Can't eat normal meals? Try about 15 grams of carbs every hour, like ½ cup juice, ½ cup regular gelatin, or 6 saltine crackers.
    • Keep taking insulin. You may even need more. Do not stop it unless your team says so.
    • Some pills may need to be paused when you can't eat or drink — often metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors. Ask your care team now, before you get sick, which ones to pause.
    • If you take an SGLT2 inhibitor or insulin and your team gave you ketone strips, check ketones when you are sick.

    Who to call

    Call your care team if:

    • Blood sugar is over 300 two checks in a row, or over 250 for more than a day
    • You have vomiting or diarrhea for more than 6 hours
    • You can't keep fluids down
    • Fever over 101°F for more than a day
    • Moderate or large ketones
    • Lows happen often, or you needed help to treat a low

    Call 911 if:

    • Passing out, seizure, or can't swallow
    • A low does not get better after 2–3 treatments
    • Very confused or hard to wake up
    • Chest pain or trouble breathing
    • Signs of stroke: face drooping, arm weakness, speech trouble

    Protecting your body

    Goals for this part
    • Know your ABCs
    • Know which checkups to get each year
    • Check your feet every day

    Over time, high blood sugar can harm the heart, kidneys, eyes, nerves, feet, and gums. The good news: regular checkups find problems early, when they are easiest to treat.

    Know your ABCs

    A — A1C

    Often less than 7%

    B — Blood pressure

    Often less than 130/80

    C — Cholesterol

    Many adults with diabetes take a statin medicine to protect the heart

    S — Stop smoking

    Free help: 1-800-QUIT-NOW (1-800-784-8669)

    My care checklist

    Check off what you have done in the last year. Any you did not check are good to ask about at your next visit.

    Daily foot care

    Diabetes can lower feeling in the feet. A small cut can turn into a big problem without you knowing.

    1. Look at the tops, bottoms, and between toes every day. Use a mirror, or ask a family member.
    2. Wash in warm (not hot) water. Test it with your elbow. Dry well between toes.
    3. Moisturize dry skin — but not between the toes.
    4. Never go barefoot, even at home. Shake out shoes before putting them on.
    5. Trim nails straight across, or have a foot doctor do it.
    Call your care team about any cut or blister that is not healing, redness, swelling, warmth, color change, or a sore that drains.
    How family can help Offer to check feet if it is hard to bend or see. Help keep track of checkup dates. Support someone who is trying to quit tobacco — quitting is hard and often takes more than one try.

    Part 9 · About 5 minutes

    Coping and support

    Living with diabetes is a lot of work, every day. It is normal to feel frustrated, worried, angry, or tired of it. This is called diabetes distress, and it is very common.

    • Talk about it with family, friends, your faith community, or your care team.
    • Ask for help. A diabetes educator, counselor, or support group can make a big difference.
    • Tell your care team if you feel down or hopeless most days for 2 weeks or more, or lose interest in things you enjoy. Depression is common and treatable.
    • Be kind to yourself. No one is perfect. Every day is a new start.
    If you are in crisis Call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline) any time, day or night. In an emergency, call 911.

    Putting it all together

    Say it in your own words

    Saying it out loud helps you remember. If someone is learning with you, take turns. Then tap to check.

    1. What would you do if your blood sugar was 62?

    Use the Rule of 15: take 15 grams of fast sugar (4 glucose tablets or ½ cup juice), wait 15 minutes, and check again. Repeat if still below 70. Then eat a snack or meal.

    2. What are 3 things to do on a sick day?

    Check blood sugar every 4 hours · drink plenty of fluids · keep taking insulin · ask which pills to pause · call your team if numbers stay high or you can't keep fluids down.

    3. What should half of your plate be?

    Non-starchy vegetables, like greens, broccoli, peppers, or tomatoes.
